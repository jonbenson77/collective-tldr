What "Auto Research" actually is

Andrej Karpathy (one of the original OpenAI founders) published a research method that lets an AI grade its own work and improve itself in a loop. Ben took that method and packaged it as a Claude skill anyone can install. The way it works in plain English: you point the skill at one of your existing skills (say, your subject-line writer). You give it 4 to 5 rules the output must follow. The skill runs your subject-line writer 5 times, scores each output against your rules, writes a hypothesis like "if I add a 50-character cap to step 4, the score goes up", tries it, keeps the change if the score went up, throws it out if it didn't. Repeats until it hits 100% or runs out of iterations. You walk away. It comes back with a smarter version of your skill plus a dashboard showing every iteration.

The 3-layer rule framework
  • Layer 1: Hard rules. Things a script can check yes or no. "Hook must be under 136 characters." "Bullet points use hyphens not asterisks." "No em dashes." These are cheap to test, fast to fix.
  • Layer 2: Pattern rules. Things an AI judge can spot. "Must follow PAS or AIDA framework." "Bold claims need a softener like 'I think' or 'I believe'." "Must include one personal story from my background file." These need a second AI agent to grade, but they capture your voice.
  • Layer 3: Tone rules. The remaining subjective layer. Most copy work fits inside layers 1 and 2 if you articulate it well. The point: even creative skills like copywriting can be 80% expressed as true/false rules. The 20% you can't pin down is where you stay in the loop.
Real numbers from Ben's tests
  • LinkedIn writer skill, 2 simple rules: baseline 80% pass rate. After 5 iterations: 100%. A 25% lift.
  • LinkedIn writer skill, 4 harder rules (hook templates, nuance words, framework adherence, personal story): baseline 68%. After 10 iterations: 95%. A 27% lift.
  • CLAUDE.md routing skill (which folder a memory goes in): 9.9% lift in 5 iterations.
  • Hard cap iterations at 10. Ben confirmed past 15 iterations the skill gets WORSE not better. It overfits to its own feedback. 5 to 10 is the sweet spot.

Pricing per 1M tokens
  • GPT 5.5: $5 input, $30 output. Most expensive of the 3. OpenAI claims it uses fewer tokens per task so end-to-end cost lands about 20% above GPT 5.4.
  • Claude Opus 4.7: $5 input, $25 output. Middle of the pack on price.
  • DeepSeek V4 Pro: $1.70 input, $3.48 output. About 8 times cheaper than the others on output. Open-source weights but the model is 1.6 trillion parameters so you can't run it locally without serious hardware. Use the API.
Benchmark winners
  • SWE-Bench Verified and SWE-Bench Pro: Opus 4.7 wins both. Real GitHub coding work. Opus stays the right tool for actual codebase changes.
  • Terminal Bench 2.0: GPT 5.5 wins by a huge margin (87.2). Even higher than Anthropic's unreleased Mythos model reportedly scores. Terminal/CLI work goes to Codex.
  • Long context (500K to 1M tokens): Opus 4.7 regressed badly. Worse than Opus 4.6 here, and worse than DeepSeek V4 and GPT 5.5 in the same range. If you ever need to dump a giant codebase or a long manuscript, GPT 5.5 is now the better pick. (Note: at that token range you're hitting context rot in any model.)
  • DeepSeek V4 Pro: never wins, but stays within 5 points of Opus on coding while being 8x cheaper. Decent fallback for simple, high-volume tasks where cost matters more than quality.
The 3JS flight sim head-to-head

Chase ran the same prompt ("build a 3JS browser flight simulator that feels good to fly") through all 3 models. Results: GPT 5.5 in Codex won fast. 7 minutes for the first pass, 66K tokens total across 3 iterations, fully playable plane that took off, instruments worked, third-person camera worked. Clear winner. Claude Code with Opus 4.7 came second. 20 minutes for first pass, 200K tokens, plane spawned in mid-air at first, took 3 prompts to make it playable. The most thorough plan of the three (5 minutes just to plan), but the most expensive and slowest. DeepSeek V4 came last. 10 minutes, 63K tokens, completely broken graphics on first pass, still incoherent on second pass. Would have needed a much more specific prompt to get anywhere usable.
